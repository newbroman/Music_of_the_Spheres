ἡ ἰδέα

The idea

For two thousand years, people believed the heavens made music. The planets were carried on great transparent spheres. Each sphere turned at its own speed, and each speed was a note. Together the notes made a harmony too perfect, or too constant, for human ears to notice. The idea is called the music of the spheres, or in Latin musica universalis.

This app takes the idea literally. Every planet, moon, dwarf planet and comet is computed from its real orbit for the date on screen, and each one sings. The god it is named after supplies the voice. How fast it moves, how big it looks, how its orbit tilts and how it spins all shape the sound. You choose where in the solar system to stand and listen.

ἱστορία

From Pythagoras to Kepler

Pythagoras: harmony is number 6th century BC

The story goes that Pythagoras heard hammers in a smithy ringing in harmony and found the reason in their proportions. Whatever the truth of the story, the discovery credited to his followers is real: the consonant intervals of music are simple ratios of whole numbers. Halve a string and it sounds an octave higher. Two-thirds of the string gives a fifth, and three-quarters a fourth. If music is number, and the heavens move by number, then the heavens might be music.

Plato: the Sirens on the whorls c. 375 BC

At the end of the Republic, Plato tells the Myth of Er. Er is a soldier killed in battle who returns to life and describes what the souls of the dead are shown. The cosmos is a great Spindle of Necessity, made of eight nested whorls that turn at different speeds. On the rim of each whorl stands a Siren, singing a single note, and the eight notes together make one harmony. The three Fates sing with them: Lachesis of the past, Clotho of the present and Atropos of the future.

On each of the circles stood a Siren, carried round with it and uttering one sound, one note; and from all eight there came the concord of a single harmony.Plato, Republic X, paraphrased

In the Timaeus, Plato goes further. The soul of the world itself is built from musical proportions, the series 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 9, 27 and the ratios that fill the gaps between them, and the circles of the planets are cut from that soul. The heavens are not merely like music: their structure is a musical scale.

Aristotle: a doubt 4th century BC

Not everyone agreed. In On the Heavens, Aristotle argued that bodies so vast moving so fast would make a sound loud enough to shatter things on Earth, and since nothing shatters, there is no sound. The Pythagoreans had an answer ready: we cannot hear the music because it has been sounding since the moment we were born, and we only notice a sound against silence.

Ptolemy: the mathematics of the heavens 2nd century AD

Claudius Ptolemy of Alexandria wrote both the standard astronomy of the ancient world and a major treatise on music, the Harmonics. In its final book he argues that the same ratios that make musical consonance also structure the soul and the heavens. In his astrological work, the Tetrabiblos, he set out the classical aspects, the significant angles between planets: conjunction, sextile, square, trine and opposition. The app joins these two ideas, letting each aspect sound a consonance (see Tuning).

Boethius: three kinds of music 6th century AD

The Roman philosopher Boethius passed the idea to medieval Europe. He described three kinds of music: musica mundana, the music of the cosmos; musica humana, the harmony of body and soul; and musica instrumentalis, the music we actually play and hear. For a thousand years after him, music was taught at universities as a mathematical science, alongside arithmetic, geometry and astronomy.

Kepler: the harmony of the world 1619

Johannes Kepler gave the idea its most precise form in Harmonices Mundi, The Harmony of the World. By then Kepler knew the planets move on ellipses, faster near the Sun and slower far from it. He measured each planet's angular speed as seen from the Sun at perihelion, its closest point, and at aphelion, its furthest point. He found that the ratios between those speeds fall close to musical intervals. Each planet therefore sings not a single note but a short rising and falling phrase. The book also contains, almost in passing, his third law of planetary motion, which links each planet's period to its distance from the Sun.

Kepler noticed that the Earth's speed changes by only about a semitone over the year. He wrote it out as the syllables mi, fa, mi and joked that they stood for misery and famine: the note of the place where we live.

The thinkers’ heavens

The app opens on a timeline of thinkers from Pythagoras to Kepler, ending with the sky as we know it today. Tap one and the dial becomes their cosmos: drawn their way from the real positions of this moment, and singing by their rules. A reading beside it gives their idea in brief, a quotation, and a note on what is faithful and what is our reconstruction. You can tap any voice to hear it alone.

Each ancient cosmos can be heard two ways. As they wrote it keeps strictly to the sources: each sphere holds its single note, Aristotle’s heavens are silent, and the lyre and the monochord are simply played through. Heard from Athens is our interpretation of how it might sound to someone standing there. The sky turns overhead once a day, so each voice rises in the east, crosses the south and sets in the west, muffled below the horizon. Each body sings again as it moves along the zodiac, with a falling ornament when it goes backward and a flourish when it enters a new sign. Ptolemy’s loops walk the notes of his tetrachords, Nicomachus’s planets pluck their strings as they move, Fludd’s choir sings over the monochord, and Aristotle’s harmony fades as your ears grow used to it, almost to silence, swelling again whenever you notice it afresh.

The thinkers fall into two camps. For Pythagoras’s followers, Cicero, Ptolemy and Boethius’s first order, the outermost sphere, spinning fastest each day, sings highest and the Moon lowest. For Nicomachus, and later Kepler, each planet’s own orbit decides: the quicker the orbit, the higher the note, so the Moon is highest and Saturn lowest. Aristotle stands apart: there is no sound at all.

The readings were checked against the sources. Where the ancient texts leave a gap, such as which Siren sings which note in Plato’s myth, the app says so and gives the commentators’ reading or its own choice.

Bharata’s heaven keeps to what the Nāṭyaśāstra (chapters 28–33, c. 200 BC – AD 200) says: seven svaras, twenty-two śrutis counted 4-3-2-4-4-3-2 in the ṣaḍja-grāma, the two grāmas, the mūrchanās and the jātis, and the experiment with two vīṇās. Bharata gives the śrutis no sizes, so the tuning is a reconstruction (the just reading set out by Thakur: Sa 1, Re 10/9, Ga 32/27, Ma 4/3, Pa 3/2, Dha 5/3, Ni 16/9). The text says nothing of planets: the svaras’ animals come from the Nāradīya Śikṣā, their gods and colours from Śārṅgadeva’s Saṅgītaratnākara (13th century), which also holds the idea of nāda-brahman, and the planets heard from Ujjain are our own mapping, the śrutis laid on the sidereal zodiac. Sources: Ghosh (1961); Rowell, Music and Musical Thought in Early India (1992); Widdess, The Rāgas of Early Indian Music (1995); Lath, A Study of Dattilam (1978); Thakur, “The Notion of Twenty-Two Shrutis”, Resonance 20 (2015).

Śārṅgadeva’s heaven keeps to the first chapter of the Saṅgītaratnākara (Devagiri, c. 1210–1247): nāda-brahman, nāda rising through the five places of the body, the twenty-two śrutis with their names and classes, shown on a vīṇā of twenty-two strings, and each svara’s colour, continent, seer, god, metre, rasa and creature. The text pairs no svara with a planet, so the sky heard from Devagiri is our mapping, and the rings follow the svaras’ order. Sources: Shringy and Sharma’s translation (1978, 1989); Kunhan Raja’s translation (1945); Subhadra Chaudhary’s edition (2000).

The Maya are a living people, and the card keeps to what the Dresden Codex counts: the 260-day count of thirteen numbers and twenty day names, the haab’ and the Long Count, and the Venus table (pages 24 and 46–50) with its stations of 236, 90, 250 and 8 days and its return after five cycles, eight years. Java is a living tradition, not a single thinker: the Javanese calendar of interlocking weeks (the five-day pasaran beside the seven-day week, the 35-day weton, the 210-day pawukon, the mancapat of directions and colours) and the gamelan’s cycles of gong, kenong, kempul and kethuk. How the sky becomes sound

Every body's position is calculated from its orbital elements for the date shown, then turned into sound by a fixed set of rules. Nothing is random except the small, natural irregularities of the singing voices.

Why pitch from speed? Speed is what Kepler used, and it is what changes as a planet moves. The app uses a gentle power law: a planet moving twice as fast as another sings a little over a third of an octave higher. That keeps the slowest outer planets within hearing while preserving every planet's order and every glide.

Tuning, modes and the zodiac

Pythagorean tuning

The app tunes its scale the way the Pythagoreans did, by stacking pure fifths (3 : 2) and folding them into one octave. Modern instruments divide the octave into twelve equal steps instead. Pythagorean fifths and fourths are perfectly pure, while its major thirds are slightly wide, which gives chords a bright, ancient edge. You can also choose Kepler: free glide, where every pitch follows the orbital speed exactly with no scale at all.

The Greek modes

A Greek mode is a pattern of tones and semitones within the octave. Apollo's drone can sit in any of seven. The ancient names were later reassigned by medieval theorists, so the Greek and modern names do not match, which can be confusing:

The Sun's sign sets the key

The zodiac is the band of twelve constellations that the Sun, Moon and planets travel through. As seen from Earth, the Sun moves through one sign a month. The app moves the key by a fifth for each sign, so over a year the music turns once around the circle of fifths. Aries, where the Sun stands at the spring equinox, is home. Libra, at the autumn equinox, is the furthest key away, and spring brings the music home again. Neighbouring signs differ by a single note, so the change is always gentle.

Aspects sing their intervals

When two planets stand at one of the classical angles as seen from where you are listening, a soft pair of notes sounds the matching interval between them, and both planets swell slightly as the angle comes exact. This is the app's reading of Ptolemy's twin ideas: the aspects of the Tetrabiblos and the consonances of the Harmonics.

Ptolemy gives a consonance only to the opposition, trine and square. The unison for the conjunction and the major sixth for the sextile are our additions, tuned like everything else in the app in Pythagorean ratios.

Clearer speaking voices

The gods speak with the voices installed on your device, so how clear they sound, and how different from each other, depends on what you have installed. The app picks the clearest voices it finds for the page's language and gives each god a different one where it can, with its own pitch and pace. A few minutes in your device's settings makes a big difference:

  • Android: open Settings → Accessibility → Text-to-speech output (on Samsung phones: Settings → General management → Text-to-speech). Choose Google's speech engine as the preferred engine, tap ⚙ beside it, then Install voice data, choose your language and download it. Android gives the browser one voice per country, so for more variety install several versions of your language, for example English (United Kingdom), (United States), (Australia) and (India): the gods then speak with different voices and accents. Close and reopen the app afterwards.
  • iPhone and iPad: open Settings → Accessibility → Spoken Content → Voices, choose your language and download several voices marked Enhanced or Premium, both male and female. The gods share them out between them.
  • Windows: the clearest voices are the Microsoft Natural voices, which the Edge browser uses automatically. In other browsers, add voices in Settings → Time & language → Speech → Manage voices.
  • Mac: open System Settings → Accessibility → Spoken Content → System voice → Manage Voices and download Enhanced or Premium voices for your language.
  • Chrome on a computer also offers Google's own online voices, which are clear, but only while you are connected.

The last page of the welcome tour, Before you start, checks which voices your device has and lets you hear one.

3D flight

Fly a ship through the solar system with the choir placed around your cockpit. Travel to any body, cruise, or rise above the plane of the planets. Among Jupiter's or Saturn's moons, the moons sing around you.

Past Neptune the flight carries on: through the heliopause at about 120 AU, where the hiss of the solar wind stops (Voyager 1 crossed it in 2012), through the Oort cloud and out to the nearest stars at their true positions, each sounding by the forty-octave rule with the lines of its spectral class, as loud as it looks from your ship, from its true direction, with its real Doppler shift (×1, ×100 or ×1000). Pulsars are heard as they are, radio sources as coloured noise, and above the disc the whole Milky Way turns below you while its rotation curve plays the harmonic series (a Dark-matter switch lets the chord sag). The chips at the bottom (Heliopause, Oort cloud, Alpha Centauri, Sirius, Vela pulsar, Galactic centre, Above the Milky Way, Home) fly you there; the readout shows your distance from the Sun and how long its light takes to reach you. The Stars group of the Travel panel sets the stars’ own volume (apart from the planets), how they sound (a tamed tone, a soft shimmer of grains, pulsing with their light, or off), whether a star sings its name as you arrive, the starquake (a sun-like star’s own oscillations, sped up, while you are parked beside it) and the volume of the dark-matter chord. On arrival a card gives the star’s kind, temperature and distance from home, with what you are hearing in a line, and buttons to orbit it (its sound sweeps around you), fly to the next star, or go home. Search for a star (in the Travel panel, or “Find a star” in the HUD menu): type a name in any of the app’s languages (Sirius, Сириус, シリウス, 天狼星, Syriusz…), a Bayer or Flamsteed designation in any spelling (α Cen, alpha Cen, Alpha Centauri, 61 Cyg), a constellation (it lists that constellation’s stars), a HIP, HD or Gliese number, a Gaia id when the large catalogue is downloaded, a pulsar or a galaxy; each suggestion shows its kind, class and distance, and you fly there with the autopilot or jump with ⤼. The Local Group: the journey continues beyond the Milky Way to about 1.5 Mpc — the Magellanic Clouds, Andromeda (M31, with M32 and M110), Triangulum (M33), the Sagittarius dwarf and some ninety dwarf galaxies, from McConnachie’s 2012 census, each drawn by its type and measured size at its true position, the spirals at their real inclinations. The Milky Way shrinks behind you; the readout turns to Mpc and millions of years. Each galaxy is a voice: the spirals’ own rotation chords (M31 flat at 250 km/s, M33 rising to 125; the Dark-matter switch works for them too), the dwarfs a soft tone of their integrated starlight, loud as they look from the ship, from their true direction, with their real radial velocities (Andromeda approaches: a tiny blueshift, exaggerated by the switch). Chips: Magellanic Clouds, Andromeda, Triangulum, Above the Local Group. Each star its own voice: every rule is stated on its card. Its register and body come from its luminosity class and radius (supergiants deep and broad, with the octaves below and slow breathing; giants the octave below; dwarfs thin and bright; white dwarfs tiny and glassy with odd partials); its timbre from its real absorption lines, audible as notches (Balmer lines for A stars, Ca II, Fe and Na D for Sun-like stars, TiO bands for M stars); a vibrato at its rotation rate (the turn raised 20 octaves; typical speeds for its class when unmeasured); breathing at its pulsation period; its companions as their own Wien notes from their own direction, the pair turning once in 20 s when the orbit is known; real variables follow their light curve, one cycle in 6 + 2 log₁₀ P seconds; and each star has its own micro-melody of its strongest lines folded into its octave, so neighbours of one type still differ. Click or tap any star (or a pulsar, a radio source, a galaxy, a label, or the nearest star’s name in the readout) for a card with Go there, Jump, Hear it alone, Spectrum and Radio. The stars are drawn in their true colours (their black-body spectrum through the CIE colour-matching functions: the Sun white with a hint of yellow, M dwarfs orange-red, B stars blue-white). Spectrum shows the star’s visible spectrum, 380–750 nm, with its absorption lines at their real wavelengths and its Planck curve with the peak marked; Hear its lines holds every absorption line as a sustained note by the forty-octave rule (the same element sings the same note in every star: Hα 656 nm → 416 Hz), the deepest lines loudest, the broad bands as clusters, the whole chord shifted by the star’s radial velocity at the Doppler setting — stars of the same kind share their lines; their motion shifts the chord — and Hear the sweep runs through the spectrum from violet (717 Hz) to red (363 Hz), the lines heard as dips. The Stars group’s Lines (chord) voice plays the nearest star as that chord as you fly. Radio shows what is honestly known of its radio emission, from a curated table of forty notable stars (sources in the credits): most stars are radio-quiet, below detection; the Sun’s bursts, the flares of Proxima Centauri, UV Ceti and AD Leonis, the SiO, H₂O and OH masers of Mira, χ Cygni, μ Cephei, Betelgeuse and Antares (each line lowered by a stated whole number of octaves: H₂O at 22 GHz −25 → 663 Hz), the free-free winds of hot stars such as ζ Puppis, Algol’s gyrosynchrotron flares; Hear the radio plays it — flares as crackling bursts with a day passing in 20 s, masers as whistling lines, quiet stars only the noise floor. Black holes: the Galactic centre is Sagittarius A*, 4.3 million suns, drawn at true scale from 2.5 au, where its shadow is 10° wide: the photon ring and the glowing flow, brighter on the side coming towards you; “Step back” shows the S-stars on their published orbits (S2, S29, S38, S55, S4716…). The hole itself is silent — the one body with no spectrum. You hear its gas (its radio-to-millimetre spectrum lowered 28 octaves as a breathing rush, flares at their real rates, X-ray flares as a hiss), a hot spot circling every 41 minutes with its Doppler and gravitational shifts, sped up ×500, and the S-stars’ voices shifted by their real velocities (S2 reaches 7,700 km/s). The galaxies’ spectra: a galaxy’s light is the sum of its stars and its glowing gas, so its Spectrum shows the dark lines of its stars and, where stars are forming, the bright emission lines of the gas they light. Old bulges and ellipticals (Andromeda’s bulge, M32, M110) look like K giants: strong Ca II H and K, the G band, Mg b, Na D and iron, weak hydrogen lines, a deep step at 400 nm. Star-forming galaxies (Triangulum, the Magellanic Clouds, IC 10, NGC 6822) add the young A stars’ hydrogen lines and bright Hα, [N II], Hβ, [O III], [O II] and [S II], heard as added notes; the dwarf spheroidals (Draco, Fornax, Sculptor, Sagittarius) are old and poor in metals, with weak metal lines. Each is a typical spectrum for its kind (Kennicutt 1992; Kinney et al. 1996), shaped by its own colour, metallicity and Hα strength. “Hear its lines” holds every line as a note by the forty-octave rule, shifted by the galaxy’s real velocity with the ×1/×100/×1000 switch (Andromeda, approaching at 300 km/s, rises); with Stars › Lines (chord) the galaxy you visit plays its lines. Bach

The Bach style writes counterpoint in the manner of a Bach invention or fugue. Aphrodite's sign composes a two-bar subject: chord tones on the beats and stepwise runs between, in quavers and semiquavers, coming home on a long note. She states it alone on the violin. Ares answers with the same line on the oboe a bar later, and Hermes adds the subject upside down on the harpsichord as a countersubject. Each voice follows the chords, so the lines move together as a sequence through the progression. Zeus walks a continuo bass on the cello under Kronos's harpsichord chords.

The sky still shapes the form. The intro is the exposition, with the voices entering one by one. A build becomes a stretto, where the answer crowds in after only half a bar. The drop becomes the climax: the full organ with its pedal, Gaia's trumpet and Apollo's timpani, as in Bach's festive cantatas. The break is an episode with the answer silent.

The other composers

Beside Bach, the Concert row has six more composers. Each is built from the way that composer actually worked, not just a change of instruments. The planets are still the band, and the sky still decides when the builds and climaxes come.

  • Mozart: an eight-bar classical period, a question ending on the dominant and an answer coming home. Zeus plays an Alberti bass on the piano and Aphrodite the violin melody, composed from her sign. Gaia's oboe follows in thirds, and Hermes' flute trills at each cadence. Horns and timpani come in at the climax.
  • Satie: a slow three in the manner of the Gymnopédies. A bass note falls on the one, then a floating seventh chord, swaying between two harmonies, under a sparse, falling melody.
  • Debussy: ninth chords that glide in parallel by whole tones, harp washes from Jupiter's moons, and a flute arabesque. The Moon chooses the scale: the whole-tone scale while she waxes, the pentatonic while she wanes.
  • Pärt: tintinnabuli. The violin melody moves step by step towards a central note, in phrases that grow by one note each time, approaching alternately from above and below. The piano answers each note with the nearest note of the home triad, a cello mirrors the line below, and a bell marks the time.
  • Reich: phasing. Three marimbas play the same twelve-note pattern. The second is shifted by the angle between Venus and the Earth, the third by Mars, so as the planets move the patterns slide against each other. The clapping is phased by the Moon.
  • Glass: additive organ arpeggios that grow by a note each bar, in threes against a left hand in twos, over an organ pedal, with a choir.

The rest of the band

The Concert row has grown beyond those seven. Hildegard sings her own twelfth-century Latin words (O virtus Sapientiae, Ave generosa, O ignis Spiritus Paracliti and others) in chant composed in her manner, one human voice over a drone: a breath before each phrase, a leap of a fifth or an octave into a long melisma, a slow descent, vibrato that blooms late, and other voices joining in organum at the climax. The Latin line is shown on the floor as it is sung. Tallis is imitative polyphony, Vivaldi a concerto whose character follows the season, Chopin a nocturne with rubato, Ravel the dawn of Daphnis et Chloé: murmuring water in fast even arpeggios, birdsong from the piccolo and a solo violin, a broad melody rising slowly through the low strings and passed upward, ninth and added-sixth chords in parallel, harp glissandi, and the aerial voices of a wordless offstage chorus, far off at first and swelling to the sunrise; no snare, only a soft timpani roll and crotales. Holst gives each section to one god's movement, Shostakovich marches and grins, Messiaen uses modes of limited transposition and birdsong, Ligeti crawls through a cluster, and Morricone is in his manner only, with none of his themes: a whistle, a twanging guitar, a whip, men shouting short syllables, chimes, a wordless soprano and a mariachi trumpet, building slowly to one huge climax. Birtwistle is in his manner too, with none of his music: ritual blocks of verse and refrain, each opened by the tam-tam and heard from a new angle, over a labyrinth of pulses in which the woodblocks, toms, tuba, bass drum and the moons’ xylophones each keep their own clock (three, five, seven and eleven steps), brass cries, the horns’ refrain, an electronic aura, and the oboe’s long cantus over everything. Beethoven is in the manner of his middle period (none of his music is quoted): a whole sonata movement grows from one short-short-short-long cell whose leap comes from Ares’ sign. The intro strikes tutti chords that fall back to pianissimo; the groove is the exposition, a stormy minor theme over agitated repeated notes with the basses answering the cell half a bar later, then a lyrical major theme from Aphrodite over an Alberti bass, announced by a horn call; the build is the development, the cell inverted, compressed and tossed between the strings and the winds as it climbs by sequence on diminished sevenths (by semitones under a square or an opposition, by tones under a trine or a sextile) to a dominant pedal and a timpani roll; the climax is the recapitulation and coda, in the major when the Moon is waxing, with trombones joining and tonic–dominant cadences hammered home; the break is a slow movement for the oboe over pizzicato, which ends with the timpani alone on the tonic. Zeus and Kronos give each phrase its chords and Poseidon the colour of its dominant, and the trumpets and timpani play only the tonic and the dominant, as in Beethoven’s day. Wagner is in his manner too (none of his music is quoted): every god has a leitmotif (Aphrodite longing, Zeus the law, Kronos fate, Gaia Erda, Ares the sword, Hermes fire, Ouranos the heavens, Poseidon the Rhine, Apollo the gold), and each returns transformed as the sky changes: in the minor under a square, inverted under an opposition, in the major under a trine, in diminution and in stretto with its partner’s in the build (the two gods in the strongest aspect), in augmentation in the climax and in fragments in the break, while its planet lights up and its name appears on the floor. The harmony reaches for the chords of Zeus, Kronos and Poseidon but seldom arrives: a half-diminished seventh slides by semitones onto a dominant seventh, which resolves deceptively, so the cadence is deferred until the climax, which ends in a plagal transfiguration. Over a slow harmonic rhythm the horns move by the smallest steps, Aphrodite’s violins spin an endless melody with appoggiaturas, the Wagner tubas sing the heavens’ chorale, the strings swell in tremolo and Erda’s double basses lie deep beneath; it opens, after the manner of Das Rheingold, on a long drone in the Sun’s key, with the horns entering one by one.

The experimental tradition has its own corner of the Concert row, each in its composer’s manner, played by the planets. Scriabin is after his Prometheus, the Poem of Fire, and the late sonatas (he died in 1915, so his manner is evoked closely): the “mystic chord” (C F♯ B♭ E A D), rooted on the Sun’s sign around the circle of fifths as his own key-to-colour wheel runs, moved by tritones and minor thirds so that it never resolves; the piano rolls it with a languid rubato, trills glitter high in the piano and the flute, the violin sings a long line on its six notes, the brass flares upward, and in the climax the organ, the choir and the bells blaze before everything dissolves into trills. Ives is after The Unanswered Question and the Universe Symphony (Ives died in 1954, so his manner is evoked closely): the outer planets’ strings play a slow, eternal, consonant chorale from the sky’s chords while Ares’ trumpet asks the same atonal question again and again; Hermes’ and Aphrodite’s flutes answer, each in a tempo of its own taken from its planet’s year against the Earth’s (four to the beat, five to three beats), calmly at first and then in growing agitation; Gaia’s horns sing Lowell Mason’s hymn tune “Nearer, My God, to Thee”, the moons chime two pulses to five beats as Saturn circles twice to Jupiter’s five, and in the climax two marching bands collide, Gaia’s with the hymn at the strings’ tempo and Zeus’ in another key marching five steps to two beats, before the flutes fall silent and the question is asked once more over the strings alone. Partch is in the manner of Harry Partch (none of his music is quoted): every pitch is a pure ratio from a tonic the Sun’s sign sets, in his 11-limit tonality diamond, otonal (1/1 9/8 5/4 11/8 3/2 7/4) while the Moon waxes and utonal as it wanes, played on his instruments made anew in synthesis: the Diamond Marimba rolling its diagonals, the Bass Marimba and the Eroica far below, the Kithara’s strummed chords and glissandi, the glassy Cloud-Chamber Bowls, the Chromelodeon’s reeds, an Adapted Viola sliding from ratio to ratio, the Boo’s bamboo ostinato, the Spoils of War’s thuds and shell casings, and a voice intoning on vowels, in a loping five for the groove and the climax and a seven for the build and the break, with a hobo-blues drawl. Cage is in his manner (none of his music is quoted): as the notes of Atlas Eclipticalis were traced from star charts, every event here takes its pitch from its planet’s longitude in the zodiac and its loudness from the planet’s brightness, so Aphrodite is loud and Poseidon barely there; a hexagram thrown from a random number seeded by the day’s sky decides, bar by bar, when each falls, how long it lasts and whether it sounds at all, and there is no beat. Nancarrow is in the manner of Conlon Nancarrow’s Studies for Player Piano (none of his music is quoted): a hard, exact player piano plays one sixteen-beat melody the sky composes in two to five voices at once, each at its own tempo, the tempi in the ratios of the planets’ orbital periods brought within an octave (Hermes 2.076, Aphrodite 1.6255, close to the golden ratio, Ares 1.063, Gaia 1) and the moons’ cell at their true 1 : 2.007 : 4.045, every note at its exact time rather than on the beat grid and each voice at its own place in the stereo; the groove has the boogie-woogie bass of the early studies, the build is a convergence canon in which Ouranos accelerates and Poseidon slows until every voice lands together on the first beat of the climax, where Apollo strikes the convergence chord and the voices run apart again, with glissandi no hand could play sweeping the keyboard. The Weather’s Song: the winds, the rain and the thunder

The Weather page plays the weather of any place on Earth, as it is now. The Greeks gave the weather its own gods, and here they sing it: the eight winds carved on the Tower of the Winds in Athens (built about 50 BC, and still standing below the Acropolis), Nephele’s clouds, Khione’s snow, Zeus’s thunder and Iris’s rainbow. Where the physics of a sound is known, the page follows it.

What you hear

  • The winds. The god of the wind’s direction sings a long vowel, from the side the wind blows from as you face: Boreas from the north, the deepest; Kaikias from the north-east; Apeliotes from the east; Euros from the south-east; Notos from the south; Lips from the south-west; Zephyros from the west, the highest and sweetest; Skiron from the north-west. When the wind lies between two of them, both sing, in proportion. A wind from behind sounds darker. A gust bends the voice up and swells it, and the gusts wander between the mean wind and the strongest gust reported.
  • The wind itself. A low roar and a howl rise with the wind’s speed, and in a gale a whistle joins them.
  • The Aeolian harp. Six gut strings of different thickness, tuned together to the sky’s low note and hung in a window, as such harps were. Air flowing past a string sheds eddies about 0.2 × speed ÷ thickness times a second (the Strouhal number), and the string sings whichever of its harmonics lies nearest. As the wind rises and falls, each string climbs and drops through its harmonics, so the harp’s chord changes with every gust.
  • Rain. A raindrop that hits water traps a small bubble of air, and the bubble rings at its own pitch, about 3.3 kHz for a bubble 1 mm across (the Minnaert frequency); most drops only tick. The heavier the rain, the more drops, and the bigger they are, so the lower they ring. Drizzle is fine, high and dense; heavy rain adds a hiss; hail rattles.
  • Ouranos, the sky. A low drone whose note is the weight of the air: A at the standard pressure of 1013 hPa, one semitone higher for every 2.5 hPa above it and lower below, so a deep depression sounds low and a high sounds high. The temperature chooses the mode of the winds’ notes and the drone: Phrygian in a frost, then Aeolian, Dorian, Mixolydian, Ionian, and Lydian in a heatwave.
  • Nephele’s clouds muffle the sky: the thicker the cloud, the duller everything becomes. Fog muffles more; fresh snow, Khione’s, hushes everything, as real snow absorbs sound. Damp air gives a longer echo, and the night is a little darker.
  • Zeus. In a thunderstorm the lightning flashes on the picture first, and the thunder follows after the time sound takes to cover the distance, at the speed of sound for the air’s temperature: a crack and a long roll when it is close, only a low rumble when it is far. On a sultry, cloudy day with a high chance of storms, Zeus may stir far off.
  • Iris and Nyx. When the sun shines on rain, Iris rings a rainbow scale and the rainbow appears. On a clear night Nyx’s stars glint, high and faint.

The words, explained

  • Solstice. The two moments of the year when the Sun reaches its farthest north (about 21 June) or its farthest south (about 21 December). For a few days around them its rising and setting points seem to stop and turn back, hence the name, from the Latin sol, sun, and sistere, to stand still. North of the equator, the June solstice brings the longest day (midsummer) and the December solstice the shortest (midwinter); south of the equator it is the other way round.
  • Equinox. About 20 March and 22 September, halfway between the solstices: day and night are nearly equal everywhere, and the Sun rises almost due east and sets almost due west.
  • Cross-quarter days. The four days halfway between a solstice and an equinox, in early February, early May, early August and early November. They split the year into eighths. In the old Celtic calendar of Ireland, Scotland, Wales and the Isle of Man they began the seasons: Imbolc (early February, the first stirring of spring; in Welsh Gŵyl Fair y Canhwyllau, in English Candlemas), Beltane (May Day, the start of summer; Calan Mai), Lughnasadh (early August, the start of the harvest; Gŵyl Awst, Lammas) and Samhain (the start of winter and the dark half of the year; Calan Gaeaf, now Hallowe’en and All Saints). They are now kept on fixed dates (1 February, 1 May, 1 August, 1 November); the page gives the astronomical days, when the Sun is exactly halfway, a few days later. Because the Sun stands at the same height in the sky on the May and the August days, they share one sunrise bearing, and the November and February days share another. Some ancient monuments are often said to face them: the passage of the Mound of the Hostages at Tara, in Ireland, is said to catch the sunrise at Samhain and Imbolc.
  • Lunar standstills. The Moon’s path is tilted about 5° to the Sun’s, and the tilt turns right round every 18.6 years. At a major standstill (the last was in 2024–25, the next will be about 2043) the Moon rises and sets farther north and south than the Sun ever does, for a year or two; at a minor standstill (next about 2034) its range is at its narrowest, inside the Sun’s. Some stone circles, such as Callanish on Lewis and the recumbent stone circles of Aberdeenshire, are thought to mark them.
  • Bearing. A direction measured clockwise from true north: 90° is east, 180° south, 270° west. True north is the direction of the North Pole; a magnetic compass can differ from it by a few degrees.
  • Horizon height. How high the skyline stands above a level horizon in the direction you look, in degrees (a hill 100 m high a kilometre away stands about 6° up). A raised skyline makes the Sun appear later and farther along its path, so sunrise moves towards the south and sunset towards the north, or the other way round south of the equator.
  • First gleam. The times and bearings are for the moment the top edge of the Sun first shows or last disappears, and they allow for the air bending the light, which lifts the Sun by about half a degree at the horizon. That is why sunrise comes a few minutes before the Sun’s centre is geometrically level with the horizon.
  • Stonehenge’s Station Stones. Four stones, numbered 91 to 94 (two survive), stood on the ring of the 56 Aubrey Holes and mark out a rectangle about 80 by 33 metres. Its short sides run parallel to the monument’s axis, towards the midsummer sunrise one way and the midwinter sunset the other. Its long sides point south-east to the southernmost moonrise and north-west to the northernmost moonset at a major lunar standstill, which comes every 18.6 years (the last in 2024–25). Only within about 50 km of Stonehenge’s latitude do those two directions cross at a right angle, so the rectangle can fit both; some take that as a sign the place was chosen for it, others think the match is chance. In the 1960s the astronomers Gerald Hawkins and Fred Hoyle argued that the 56 Aubrey Holes, with markers moved round them, could have predicted eclipses (56 is close to three cycles of 18.6 years), but most archaeologists doubt it: the holes held cremations and probably bluestones. On the map the stones are drawn in approximate positions from the published plan; compare their sides with the solstice and standstill lines drawn from the centre.
  • Bright stars and the heliacal rising. A star rises and sets at the same two points on the horizon every night, so its rising point can be marked like the Sun’s. For some weeks each year it is lost in the Sun’s glare; the first morning it shows again, low in the east just before dawn, is its heliacal rising, and many peoples kept time by it. Sirius’s told the Egyptians the Nile flood was near and began their year; the Pleiades and Orion’s Belt still time the farming seasons of Java’s pranata mangsa; the Babylonians listed dozens of such risings; Canopus, the second-brightest star, rises far in the south. The lines show where each star rises and sets, 1° above a level horizon; the dates are approximate, to a few days, because they depend on the clearness of the air and the watcher’s eyes.
  • Era. The sky shifts slowly. The Earth’s tilt, which sets how far north and south the Sun and Moon swing, was about 24° when the pyramids and Stonehenge were built and is 23.4° today, so the solstice lines have moved by about a degree. The stars move far more: the Earth’s axis wobbles round once in about 26 000 years (precession), so each star’s rising point drifts, and Sirius, the Pleiades and Orion rose in quite different places five thousand years ago. Move the slider to a monument’s own century to see its sky as its builders saw it: the Sun’s and Moon’s lines, the stars and their heliacal risings all follow.

Why they mattered

  • A calendar in the sky. Before written calendars, the Sun’s turning points were the surest way to keep track of the year: when to sow and harvest, when to move the herds, how long the winter stores had to last. Anyone could see them by watching where the Sun rose against the skyline, day after day, until it stopped and turned back. A stone or a passage set on that line fixed the day for good, and for everyone.
  • Midwinter: the return of the light. For farmers in the north, the shortest day was the hinge of survival, the moment the Sun stopped retreating and began to come back. Several of the great monuments are built for it. At Newgrange, in Ireland, the rising Sun shines down the 19-metre passage to the inner chamber for a few mornings around the winter solstice; at Maeshowe, on Orkney, the setting Sun does the same. Stonehenge’s main axis runs from the midsummer sunrise one way to the midwinter sunset the other, and many archaeologists now think midwinter was the greater occasion: the bones of pigs at nearby Durrington Walls show they were slaughtered for feasts in midwinter. In Anglesey, the passage of Bryn Celli Ddu catches the sunrise at midsummer.
  • The equinoxes. Day and night are equal, but the Sun’s rising point moves fastest at the equinoxes and the day is hard to fix by eye, so prehistoric monuments seem to mark it less often. They mattered greatly to later calendars: the date of Easter is reckoned from the spring equinox, the Persian new year, Nowruz, begins at it, and at Chichén Itzá in Mexico the setting Sun at the equinoxes casts a serpent of light down the pyramid’s stair.
  • The cross-quarter days: the farming year. They marked the turns of a herding life. At Beltane the cattle were driven out to the summer pastures between bonfires; at Lughnasadh the first harvest was gathered and people met at fairs and games; at Samhain the herds came in, animals that could not be fed through the winter were killed, and the dead were thought to come close; at Imbolc the ewes began to give milk and lamb. Most of this is known from medieval Irish writing and later folk custom, and how far back it reaches is debated, but the festivals were kept in Wales, Scotland and Ireland for many centuries (Calan Mai and Calan Gaeaf among them).
  • The Moon’s long cycle. The Moon gave the months and the light to travel and work by at night. Its 18.6-year swing between standstills can only be seen over half a lifetime of watching, so a monument that marks it suggests generations of careful observation handed down. At Callanish on Lewis, at a major standstill, the Moon skims low along the southern hills and seems to set and then gleam again among the stones; the recumbent stone circles of Aberdeenshire frame the setting Moon in the south between two upright stones. How deliberate such alignments were is still argued over.
  • What we cannot know. These people left no writing. The alignments show that they watched the sky closely and built it into their most important places; what the moments meant to them we infer from the monuments themselves, from what was found in and around them, and from the folklore of much later times.

φῶς

Light and radio

Light and sound are both vibrations, and an octave means the same for both: twice the frequency. Lower a colour of light by forty octaves, dividing its frequency by 240 (about 1.1 million million), and it lands in hearing. The A above middle C, 440 Hz, raised forty octaves is 4.84 × 1014 Hz: light of 620 nm, an orange-red. The whole of visible light, from violet at 380 nm to deep red at about 750 nm, spans less than one octave; the ear hears about ten. This one rule, forty octaves, is used for the two light layers below. Light, in the Choir section of the dial and in the flight's Navigation panel, has three switches: Colour is on by default, Heat and Radio are off, and the choice is remembered. Tap a body to hear it alone and its light layers come with it, and the information card shows a line such as “Mars · reflects red, absorbs blue · heat 14 µm → a 19.8 Hz pulse”.

Colour: the planet's reflected light shapes its voice

Each body has a measured reflectance spectrum: how much of each wavelength it sends back, from the near ultraviolet at 350 nm to the near infrared at 1000 nm. Brought down forty octaves, that span runs from about 780 Hz to 270 Hz, and it becomes a filter on the body's voice, lifting the colours it reflects and cutting those it absorbs, by up to 12 dB. A voice's harmonics cover many octaves and the light covers one and a half, so the curve is folded by octaves the way notes are folded into pitch classes: the visible octave, 380–760 nm, is the reference, a near-infrared wavelength is halved into it (890 nm sits with 445 nm, as a note an octave down keeps its name) and the near ultraviolet is doubled. Each octave of light is set about its own mean first, so the infrared adds its bands without darkening the blue it folds onto, and the one-octave curve is then applied to every octave of the voice. Mars, which reflects red and absorbs blue, becomes warmer and darker. Uranus and Neptune take audible notches where methane absorbs, at 619 nm, 727 nm and 890 nm (and 543 nm for Neptune): the 619 nm band lands almost exactly on A440. Jupiter's and Saturn's weaker methane bands and Venus's bright yellowish clouds each colour their voices. The asteroids and dwarf planets use class spectra (a dark grey C-type, a reddish S-type, bright ice, cold red bodies) and comets the dark red of a comet's nucleus. On phones the curve is smoothed more coarsely, and in both cases it is applied as one short filter rather than a rack of equalisers. It changes the timbre, not the loudness: a flat voice passes at the same level. Spectrum, on a body’s information card on the dial (and the λ beside each moon in a system), in the flight’s readout, or by tapping a planet or moon in flight, shows its reflected light from 350 nm to 2.5 µm, the Sun’s own lines faint and the body’s own bands bold (methane, water ice, pyroxene, a comet’s glowing C₂ and CN), and Hear its lines holds those bands as a chord by the same forty-octave rule, unfolded, so an infrared band is simply a lower note (2 µm → 136 Hz), over a quiet bed of the Sun’s lines; Hear the sweep plays the light from ultraviolet to infrared.

Spectral lines: the bodies sing their own light

In the dial’s Voices section, Sound as chooses Gods’ voices (as before), Spectral lines or Both, and the choice is remembered. With Spectral lines every body that sings, in Today’s sky and in every thinker’s heavens, keeps its music and changes its voice: its pitch, rhythm and phrasing stay the same, by that thinker’s rules, but it sounds as its own line chord. The chord is made of the strongest lines in the body’s spectrum: its own bands, filled with the Sun’s lines in its reflected light when it has few of its own (so the Moon is reflected sunlight with its slope and pyroxene); the Sun sounds its Fraunhofer lines, and in the thinkers’ heavens each sphere sounds the body it carries (the fixed stars the Balmer lines of a white star like Vega, the elements the Earth’s bands; the Fates keep their voices). The rule: each line is placed by the forty-octave rule, then the whole chord is moved, keeping the real ratios between its lines, so that its centre, the loudness-weighted average of the lines’ pitches, sits on the note the body is singing; a line that would leave 35 Hz–6 kHz moves by whole octaves. With At their true pitch the lines stay where the forty-octave rule puts them (350 nm → 779 Hz, 2.5 µm → 109 Hz), so only the rhythm and phrasing of the music remain. Both lets the gods sing with their lines sounding under them.

To stay light on a phone each body sounds only its strongest lines, three in Eco quality, five in Standard and eight in High, as sines that share one pitch source, and the number of voices is the gods’ own budget. In Today’s sky the chords breathe in their singers’ phrases; in the thinkers’ heavens they are struck and swell on the thinker’s articulations, and the lyre and the monochord strike the chord instead of a string. The Colour filter steps aside while the bodies sound as their lines, because the lines already are their light. The Spectrum sheet now sings as it opens: its lines held, gently breathing and looping, until you press Stop or close the sheet (the stars’ sheet in the flight does the same); Hear the sweep plays the light from ultraviolet to infrared and then returns to the held chord. The sheet follows the page’s volume, stays silent when the volume is at zero, and lowers the page’s music a little while it sounds.

Heat: the planet's own warmth as a bass or a throb

Every body glows in the infrared, and Wien's law gives the wavelength where it glows most: 2898 µm·K divided by its temperature. Forty octaves down, that peak becomes a pitch. Earth, at 255 K, peaks at 11.4 µm, which is 24 Hz: a sine hum at the bottom of hearing, with a quieter second harmonic so that a phone's small speaker can hear it too. Mercury (440 K on average; its day side reaches 700 K) hums at 41 Hz and Venus's cloud tops (230 K, though its surface is 737 K) at 22 Hz. Below 20 Hz the peak is too slow to hear as a note, so it becomes a throb, an amplitude pulse at that rate on a soft low drone a few octaves up: Mars (210 K) throbs at 19.8 Hz, Jupiter (124 K) at 11.7 Hz, Saturn (95 K) at 8.9 Hz, Uranus and Neptune (59 K) at 5.6 Hz, Pluto (44 K) at 4.1 Hz. The loudness follows the body's thermal output as seen from where you are listening, compressed into musical limits; Jupiter radiates 1.7 times the energy it receives from the Sun, Saturn 1.8 and Neptune 2.6, so their heat is relatively stronger. The layer is kept quiet, goes through the same automatic gain and limiter as the choir, and is left out of the score and MIDI.

Radio: the planets that transmit

Some planets send out real radio waves, and radio astronomers listen to them through a receiver, which turns them into sound by demodulation, not by lowering their pitch: there is no forty-octave rule here, and the textures are a receiver's noise, not tones. Each one follows the real sky of the moment. Jupiter's decametric storms (10–40 MHz) depend on where Io is in its orbit and on which side of Jupiter faces you: the Io-A, B, C and D source regions, roughly as catalogued, are computed from the app's Io and from Jupiter's System III rotation of 9 h 55 m 29.7 s, corrected for light time. In a storm you hear L-bursts, a slow surf of noise, and S-bursts, rapid crackles and pops; between storms a quieter hectometric hiss. Saturn's kilometric radiation is whistling chirps and squeaks that wax and wane with its 10.7-hour rotation. Earth's auroral kilometric radiation is rising and falling chirps, like the whistlers and chorus of the magnetosphere, stronger from the night side and in active spells, whose weather here is random. Uranus and Neptune give faint short bursts in one sector of their rotations, 17.2 and 16.1 hours. Radio is heard only where the planet's voice is heard, scaled to it, and never enters the score.

ὁ μέγας ἐνιαυτός

The Galactic year

The Sun goes round the Milky Way once in about 230 million years, a galactic year, and so do its neighbours, each on its own orbit. Because the Galaxy’s rotation curve is flat, a star at half the distance goes round twice as often and one at a third three times: the orbits play the harmonic series. No orbit is a circle. Each star has three motions: it goes round (Ω), swings in and out about its guiding circle (κ, the epicycle) and bobs up and down through the disc (ν). For the Sun, from the app’s own model of the Galaxy: once in 228, 162 and 78 million years (Ω 27.0, κ 37.9, ν 78.6 km/s/kpc; it swings 0.5 kpc in and out and rises about 100 pc above and below the plane). The central bar (turning at 39 km/s/kpc) and the spiral arms (25 km/s/kpc) are patterns that turn more slowly than most stars; where a star’s swing keeps time with a pattern passing it, or it turns with the pattern, it is locked into the pattern’s rhythm — the Hercules stream, stars passing us about 50 km/s behind the rest, is shaped this way by the bar. Kepler’s simple ratios return at the scale of the Galaxy.

Where to hear it. In the Flight, above the Milky Way, a card offers Play the Galactic year (also a button under the dark-matter switch in the Travel panel’s Stars group). The Sun (gold), real neighbours from the star catalogue (blue) and three stars of the Hercules stream (red) run round on their orbits, computed in the app’s model of the Galaxy, with rosette trails and their heights exaggerated ten times (as the card says); the bar and the arms turn at their own speeds, and Seen: turning with the bar holds the bar still. Choose one galactic year in 8, 16, 32 or 64 s (16 s is 14.3 million years a second); the card shows the Galactic date, where the Sun is, when it crosses the plane or a spiral arm, and which stars are near a resonance with the bar. On the Galactic Synthesiser choose the Galactic year preset (a strip of its own, timed by the time speed), and in Meditate The Galactic year (one galactic year in 32 s).

The rules. Each star’s three frequencies are raised 60 octaves, the same for every star, so their ratios are true: the Sun’s chord is 160, 225 and 468 Hz. Each neighbour sounds its chord when it swings closest to the centre, the Sun once a lap, so inner stars sound higher and more often. The Sun holds its chord softly underneath: it swells as the Sun crosses the plane, where the disc is densest, and its orbit note wobbles with its swing (a quarter of the true change); crossing a spiral arm is a soft shimmer. The bar pulses each time an end sweeps past the line from the centre through today’s Sun, the arms as one sweeps today’s place of the Sun; stars near a resonance with the bar also tick when it passes them. Each star is heard from its direction.

ἡ τοπικὴ ὁμάς

The Local Group year

The Milky Way is not alone: it and Andromeda, 2.5 million light years away and coming towards us at about 110 km/s, are the two great spirals of the Local Group, with the smaller Triangulum beside Andromeda and a train of dwarf galaxies round each, among them the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds and the Sagittarius dwarf. What you see. In the Flight, press ♪ Local Group year in the travel ribbon (or in the card that appears above the Local Group): the ship flies up, looks straight down on the plane of their motion, and ten billion years of the group play as music. Each galaxy is a dot sized by its mass with a fading trail (the Milky Way gold, Andromeda blue-white, Triangulum pale blue, the Magellanic Clouds red-orange, the Sagittarius dwarf violet). The card gives the date, the Milky Way–Andromeda distance and each event as it sounds; choose the speed (¼, ½, 1 or 2 billion years a second; ¼, the default, plays the whole run in 40 s), pause, or tap a dot for its name.

Two futures. Whether the Milky Way and Andromeda collide depends on Andromeda’s sideways motion, which is very hard to measure, and on the galaxies’ masses; following thousands of possible starting points, Sawala et al. (2025) find about a 50 % chance of a merger within 10 billion years. The card offers one future of each kind: They merge (a heavier Andromeda moving as Hubble measured it: a first pass at 5.2 billion years and a single galaxy by about 8) and A near miss (Andromeda’s middle mass moving as Gaia measured it, a little more sideways: they pass 7.3 billion years from now, 250 kpc apart, and part). Both are illustrations within the uncertainties, not predictions. The model is six galaxies pulling on each other with dynamical friction and no tidal stripping; in the first future Triangulum also falls into the Milky Way (published simulations show both outcomes), the Large Magellanic Cloud merges with the Milky Way at about 4 billion years (Cautun et al. 2019 give about 2.4) and the Small Magellanic Cloud sinks into it within about 2 billion years, so it strikes only once.

The rules. Each satellite’s orbit is a rhythm: a soft strike every time it passes closest to its host (the Sagittarius dwarf round the Milky Way about every 0.7 billion years, the Small Magellanic Cloud round the Large, Triangulum round Andromeda). The pitch is the orbit’s frequency raised 63 octaves, the same for all, so the ratios are true: an orbit of a billion years is 292 Hz. The Milky Way and Andromeda each hold a drone, the first ring of their rotation chord; Andromeda’s sits above the Milky Way’s by a semitone for every 50 kpc between them, so the interval closes as they approach, both swell at a close pass, and when they merge the two drones become one. The Large Magellanic Cloud’s fall is a slow glide (its angle round the Milky Way, raised 63 octaves) that rises as it sinks. Each galaxy is heard from where it is on the screen, and the sound is kept calm, with soft attacks and long decays.

Your phone and the sound

Every voice in the app is computed live, as you listen, by your phone’s processor: nothing is a recording except the gods’ spoken words and the band’s instruments. The heaviest moments are the Flight among the stars, the Synth and the Dance; the dial and Meditate are lighter. A phone that cannot keep up does not slow down politely: the sound crackles, stutters or, on some phones, stops altogether.

The four qualities

  • High: a rich concert-hall reverb and 3D sound for headphones. For computers.
  • Standard: all the voices, with 3D placement around you (best with headphones). For recent phones.
  • Eco: fewer voices, a light echo, and the voices placed simply left and right instead of in 3D. For most mid-range and older phones.
  • Lightest: a few voices only, no echo, simple left–right placement, nothing extra. For phones that crackle even on Eco.

The app watches its own sound clock. If your phone falls behind, it steps the quality down by itself (High → Standard → Eco → Lightest) and says so; on Lightest it also asks for a larger sound buffer the next time the sound starts. You can choose the quality yourself in Stage → Quality, or in ⋯ → Sound check → Quality.

Options your phone cannot use are greyed out, with the reason beside them: a feature its browser lacks (recording, MIDI, 5.1 surround, the microphone for Listen, spoken voices in your language), or a quality the app has measured as too heavy for it. A measured limit is not final: Measure again clears it, for instance after you turn off battery saver.

A rough guide

  • Recent mid-range and flagship phones (about 2021 onwards: Snapdragon 7 and 8 series, Google Tensor, the newer Dimensity chips, iPhone 12 or later): Standard. Wear headphones for the 3D choir.
  • Older mid-range phones (about 2018–2020: Snapdragon 6 series, Helio P and G chips, 3–4 GB of memory): Eco. The app will usually choose it for you.
  • Budget, rugged and older phones (2 GB of memory, older chips, or phones that crackle on Eco): Lightest with a larger buffer. A Blackview BV9900E (Helio P90), for example, plays smoothly only on Lightest with a larger buffer.
  • Computers and tablets: High or Standard.

If the sound is silent, choppy or crackles

  1. Open ⋯ → Sound check on the page where it happens and press Play a test tone. If the tone is silent too, check the media volume and that the phone is not on silent or connected to a Bluetooth speaker you cannot hear.
  2. If the tone is clear but the music is not, set Quality to Lightest.
  3. If it still crackles, set Sound route to a larger output buffer. The sound starts a fraction of a second later, but plays smoothly on phones whose sound buffer is too small.
  4. On a few phones the sound only plays while the app is in the background. Set Sound route to through the media player, which sends the sound the way the background does.
  5. Close other apps that play or record sound, and turn off battery saver while you listen.
  6. If nothing helps, press Copy in the Sound check and send the report: it says exactly what your phone did.

Listening with the screen off

Android slows down apps it thinks are idle. If the music stutters or stops with the screen off, open your phone’s Settings → Apps, find Chrome (or Music of the Spheres, if it is listed), then Battery, and choose Unrestricted. Some phones (Xiaomi, Huawei, Oppo, Samsung) have their own extra battery manager: allow the app to run in the background there too.

Headphones, Bluetooth and the stage

  • Wired headphones give the clearest 3D placement on Standard and High.
  • Bluetooth adds a delay of a fifth of a second or more: fine for listening, noticeable when you play the Synth live.
  • For a PA or a projector, use a cable (USB audio or a headphone lead) rather than Bluetooth, keep the phone on its charger, and choose Standard if the phone can manage it.

Tips and troubleshooting

  • Wear headphones. Positions, elevation and the turning of the choir as you move are much clearer.
  • No sound? Browsers only allow sound after you tap, so press Begin the harmony, Play or a planet. Check that your phone isn't on silent.
  • In the phone app, if a phone plays no sound, check the media volume and that the phone is not on silent; if it still stays silent, open the website in your phone’s browser instead.
  • Crackling or silence on a phone: see Your phone and the sound.
  • Record captures exactly what you hear on any of the three pages. Press it to start (it starts the sound too) and again to stop. You can then play the recording back and save it as an audio file (.m4a on most phones and computers, .webm on some) to listen to anywhere. Spoken introductions come from your device's speech voice, so they are not included. Ringtone, beside it, captures the next 3, 30 or 60 seconds as a WAV file for your phone's notification, ringtone or alarm sound, and shows where to set it (Android picks the file in Settings → Sound; an iPhone needs GarageBand or a computer).
  • Each page remembers its own volume, so the 2D dial, 3D flight and dance can sit at different levels.
  • Speeding up time brings out the glides, the moons' melodies and the turning of the key through the year.
  • The first version is kept unchanged as a benchmark: v1 dial and v1 flight.

Data, accuracy and reading

  • Planet positions use NASA JPL's approximate Keplerian elements, valid to a fraction of a degree from about 1800 to 2050.
  • Dwarf planets, asteroids and comets use rounded elements, so their positions are illustrative rather than exact.
  • Every moon with an official IAU name is included, 172 of the about 465 known, with Makemake’s still-unnamed MK2. The major moons run on circular orbits in their planet’s equatorial plane; the others on their real orbits from JPL’s mean elements, many of them tilted, stretched and backwards. The small moons of the giant planets sing softly, and only the most audible moons sing at once (six on a phone, four at Eco). Moon orbits on the dial are not to scale.
  • The zodiac constellations are drawn from the catalogued positions of their main stars, converted to the ecliptic. The key follows the tropical signs, measured from the spring equinox, as astrologers use them.
  • On the 2D dial, distances are logarithmic so that Mercury and the Kuiper belt fit on one screen.
