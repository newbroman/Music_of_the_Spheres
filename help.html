ἡ ἰδέα

The idea

For two thousand years, people believed the heavens made music. The planets were carried on great transparent spheres. Each sphere turned at its own speed, and each speed was a note. Together the notes made a harmony too perfect, or too constant, for human ears to notice. The idea is called the music of the spheres, or in Latin musica universalis.

This app takes the idea literally. Every planet, moon, dwarf planet and comet is computed from its real orbit for the date on screen, and each one sings. The god it is named after supplies the voice. How fast it moves, how big it looks, how its orbit tilts and how it spins all shape the sound. You choose where in the solar system to stand and listen.

ἱστορία

From Pythagoras to Kepler

Pythagoras: harmony is number 6th century BC

The story goes that Pythagoras heard hammers in a smithy ringing in harmony and found the reason in their proportions. Whatever the truth of the story, the discovery credited to his followers is real: the consonant intervals of music are simple ratios of whole numbers. Halve a string and it sounds an octave higher. Two-thirds of the string gives a fifth, and three-quarters a fourth. If music is number, and the heavens move by number, then the heavens might be music.

Plato: the Sirens on the whorls c. 375 BC

At the end of the Republic, Plato tells the Myth of Er. Er is a soldier killed in battle who returns to life and describes what the souls of the dead are shown. The cosmos is a great Spindle of Necessity, made of eight nested whorls that turn at different speeds. On the rim of each whorl stands a Siren, singing a single note, and the eight notes together make one harmony. The three Fates sing with them: Lachesis of the past, Clotho of the present and Atropos of the future.

On each of the circles stood a Siren, carried round with it and uttering one sound, one note; and from all eight there came the concord of a single harmony.Plato, Republic X, paraphrased

In the Timaeus, Plato goes further. The soul of the world itself is built from musical proportions, the series 1, 2, 3, 4, 8, 9, 27 and the ratios that fill the gaps between them, and the circles of the planets are cut from that soul. The heavens are not merely like music: their structure is a musical scale.

Aristotle: a doubt 4th century BC

Not everyone agreed. In On the Heavens, Aristotle argued that bodies so vast moving so fast would make a sound loud enough to shatter things on Earth, and since nothing shatters, there is no sound. The Pythagoreans had an answer ready: we cannot hear the music because it has been sounding since the moment we were born, and we only notice a sound against silence.

Ptolemy: the mathematics of the heavens 2nd century AD

Claudius Ptolemy of Alexandria wrote both the standard astronomy of the ancient world and a major treatise on music, the Harmonics. In its final book he argues that the same ratios that make musical consonance also structure the soul and the heavens. In his astrological work, the Tetrabiblos, he set out the classical aspects, the significant angles between planets: conjunction, sextile, square, trine and opposition. The app joins these two ideas, letting each aspect sound a consonance (see Tuning).

Boethius: three kinds of music 6th century AD

The Roman philosopher Boethius passed the idea to medieval Europe. He described three kinds of music: musica mundana, the music of the cosmos; musica humana, the harmony of body and soul; and musica instrumentalis, the music we actually play and hear. For a thousand years after him, music was taught at universities as a mathematical science, alongside arithmetic, geometry and astronomy.

Kepler: the harmony of the world 1619

Johannes Kepler gave the idea its most precise form in Harmonices Mundi, The Harmony of the World. By then Kepler knew the planets move on ellipses, faster near the Sun and slower far from it. He measured each planet's angular speed as seen from the Sun at perihelion, its closest point, and at aphelion, its furthest point. He found that the ratios between those speeds fall close to musical intervals. Each planet therefore sings not a single note but a short rising and falling phrase. The book also contains, almost in passing, his third law of planetary motion, which links each planet's period to its distance from the Sun.

Kepler noticed that the Earth's speed changes by only about a semitone over the year. He wrote it out as the syllables mi, fa, mi and joked that they stood for misery and famine: the note of the place where we live.

οἱ φιλόσοφοι

The thinkers’ heavens

The app opens on a timeline of thinkers from Pythagoras to Kepler, ending with the sky as we know it today. Tap one and the dial becomes their cosmos: drawn their way from the real positions of this moment, and singing by their rules. A reading beside it gives their idea in brief, a quotation, and a note on what is faithful and what is our reconstruction. You can tap any voice to hear it alone.

Each ancient cosmos can be heard two ways. As they wrote it keeps strictly to the sources: each sphere holds its single note, Aristotle’s heavens are silent, and the lyre and the monochord are simply played through. Heard from Athens is our interpretation of how it might sound to someone standing there. The sky turns overhead once a day, so each voice rises in the east, crosses the south and sets in the west, muffled below the horizon. Each body sings again as it moves along the zodiac, with a falling ornament when it goes backward and a flourish when it enters a new sign. Ptolemy’s loops walk the notes of his tetrachords, Nicomachus’s planets pluck their strings as they move, Fludd’s choir sings over the monochord, and Aristotle’s harmony fades as your ears grow used to it, almost to silence, swelling again whenever you notice it afresh.

The thinkers fall into two camps. For Pythagoras’s followers, Cicero, Ptolemy and Boethius’s first order, the outermost sphere, spinning fastest each day, sings highest and the Moon lowest. For Nicomachus, and later Kepler, each planet’s own orbit decides: the quicker the orbit, the higher the note, so the Moon is highest and Saturn lowest. Aristotle stands apart: there is no sound at all.

ἡ φωνή τοῦ οὐρανοῦ

How the sky becomes sound

Every body's position is calculated from its orbital elements for the date shown, then turned into sound by a fixed set of rules. Nothing is random except the small, natural irregularities of the singing voices.

What the sky doesWhat you hearHow
Orbital speedPitchThe angular speed around the Sun, measured each moment, sets the pitch. Faster means higher, scaled so the planets span about four octaves from Mercury down to Neptune, with Earth set by the Earth's note slider.
Apparent sizeLoudnessThe body's radius divided by its distance from you: how big it looks in your sky. Too faint to hear, a voice stops running entirely.
Direction from youPositionEach voice is placed around your head where the body is: left, right, ahead, behind, above or below.
Orbital inclinationElevation and swayAs an inclined body rises above the plane of the planets it sounds higher in space and brighter in tone, and its pitch sways with it.
RotationPulseA planet's day sets a gentle pulse. Bodies that spin backward, Venus, Uranus and Pluto, pulse in reversed swells.
Axial tiltWobbleA tilted spinner wobbles in pitch with its day. Uranus, lying on its side, wobbles most.
Dwarf planets and asteroidsPerturbationBy default they do not sing. Each one tugs the pitch of the nearest planet, more strongly the larger and closer it is, like a small gravitational pull.
MoonsMelodyEach moon's phase, its angle against the Sun, walks an arch through the scale: home note at new moon, the octave at full moon, and back.
CometsBreathA comet's size is its coma, which grows sharply as it nears the Sun, so comets fade in as soft whispers and vanish again as they leave.
The SunDroneApollo, leader of the Muses, holds the home note and its fifth.

Why pitch from speed? Speed is what Kepler used, and it is what changes as a planet moves. The app uses a gentle power law: a planet moving twice as fast as another sings a little over a third of an octave higher. That keeps the slowest outer planets within hearing while preserving every planet's order and every glide.

ἁρμονία

Tuning, modes and the zodiac

Pythagorean tuning

The app tunes its scale the way the Pythagoreans did, by stacking pure fifths (3 : 2) and folding them into one octave. Modern instruments divide the octave into twelve equal steps instead. Pythagorean fifths and fourths are perfectly pure, while its major thirds are slightly wide, which gives chords a bright, ancient edge. You can also choose Kepler: free glide, where every pitch follows the orbital speed exactly with no scale at all.

The Greek modes

A Greek mode is a pattern of tones and semitones within the octave. Apollo's drone can sit in any of seven. The ancient names were later reassigned by medieval theorists, so the Greek and modern names do not match, which can be confusing:

The Sun's sign sets the key

The zodiac is the band of twelve constellations that the Sun, Moon and planets travel through. As seen from Earth, the Sun moves through one sign a month. The app moves the key by a fifth for each sign, so over a year the music turns once around the circle of fifths. Aries, where the Sun stands at the spring equinox, is home. Libra, at the autumn equinox, is the furthest key away, and spring brings the music home again. Neighbouring signs differ by a single note, so the change is always gentle.

Aspects sing their intervals

When two planets stand at one of the classical angles as seen from where you are listening, a soft pair of notes sounds the matching interval between them, and both planets swell slightly as the angle comes exact. This is the app's reading of Ptolemy's twin ideas: the aspects of the Tetrabiblos and the consonances of the Harmonics.

Ptolemy gives a consonance only to the opposition, trine and square. The unison for the conjunction and the major sixth for the sextile are our additions, tuned like everything else in the app in Pythagorean ratios.

Clearer speaking voices

The gods speak with the voices installed on your device, so how clear they sound, and how different from each other, depends on what you have installed. The app picks the clearest voices it finds for the page's language and gives each god a different one where it can, with its own pitch and pace. A few minutes in your device's settings makes a big difference:

  • Android: open Settings → Accessibility → Text-to-speech output (on Samsung phones: Settings → General management → Text-to-speech). Choose Google's speech engine as the preferred engine, tap ⚙ beside it, then Install voice data, choose your language and download it. Android gives the browser one voice per country, so for more variety install several versions of your language, for example English (United Kingdom), (United States), (Australia) and (India): the gods then speak with different voices and accents. Close and reopen the app afterwards.
  • iPhone and iPad: open Settings → Accessibility → Spoken Content → Voices, choose your language and download several voices marked Enhanced or Premium, both male and female. The gods share them out between them.
  • Windows: the clearest voices are the Microsoft Natural voices, which the Edge browser uses automatically. In other browsers, add voices in Settings → Time & language → Speech → Manage voices.
  • Mac: open System Settings → Accessibility → Spoken Content → System voice → Manage Voices and download Enhanced or Premium voices for your language.
  • Chrome on a computer also offers Google's own online voices, which are clear, but only while you are connected.

The last page of the welcome tour, Before you start, checks which voices your device has and lets you hear one.

Using the 2D dial

  • Begin the harmony starts the sound. Drag the dial to turn it, and scroll or pinch to zoom.
  • Listening post: stand at the Sun, at any planet (standing on Earth gives the old Earth-centred view) or on a comet, and choose which way you face.
  • Tap a body to hear it alone with its moons. On its card, Enter the system zooms into a planet and its moons as a miniature solar system.
  • Voices switches between Instruments and Human choir, and lets the dwarf planets either wobble the planets or sing as children.
  • Harmony holds the tuning, Apollo's mode, the zodiac key, the aspects, the size-and-distance law, the temple echo and the volume.

Flying in 3D

  • Look by dragging. On a computer, W/S fly forward and back, A/D slide, R/F rise and sink, Q/E roll, Shift boosts, C cruises and T opens Travel. On a phone, use the ▲ ▼ buttons.
  • Travel flies you to any body by autopilot. Once you arrive you stay in orbit with it.
  • Leaving: the strip along the bottom offers ◀ inward to the next planet toward the Sun, outward ▶, Cruise ahead and Rise above.
  • Speed adapts to what is near you: slow beside a planet, many times the speed of light between them.
  • Planets and moons are drawn larger than life so you can find them. Distances along their orbits are true.

The dance floor

  • Play, then pick a club style, Trance (138 BPM, minor), House (124 BPM, the modern Dorian), Jungle (166 BPM, chopped breakbeats and a rolling Reese bass) or Techno (130 BPM, the dark modern Phrygian), or one of the ballroom and Latin styles below.
  • Sky speed sets how many days of orbital motion pass per bar. A faster sky brings more aspects, so more builds and drops.
  • The progression is home, then the chords on which Zeus, Kronos and Poseidon stand in the zodiac as seen from Earth.
  • The band lists which planet plays which instrument. It changes with the style. Mute any of them.

Each ballroom and Latin style is played by the kind of band you would hear on the dance floor, using recorded instruments that load when you choose the style. The same sky still drives them: the chords come from the planets' signs, Jupiter's moons keep their 1 : 2 : 4 resonance, and aspects bring builds and drops. If the recordings cannot load, synthesised instruments stand in.

Bach

The Bach style writes counterpoint in the manner of a Bach invention or fugue. Aphrodite's sign composes a two-bar subject: chord tones on the beats and stepwise runs between, in quavers and semiquavers, coming home on a long note. She states it alone on the violin. Ares answers with the same line on the oboe a bar later, and Hermes adds the subject upside down on the harpsichord as a countersubject. Each voice follows the chords, so the lines move together as a sequence through the progression. Zeus walks a continuo bass on the cello under Kronos's harpsichord chords.

The sky still shapes the form. The intro is the exposition, with the voices entering one by one. A build becomes a stretto, where the answer crowds in after only half a bar. The drop becomes the climax: the full organ with its pedal, Gaia's trumpet and Apollo's timpani, as in Bach's festive cantatas. The break is an episode with the answer silent.

The other composers

Beside Bach, the Concert row has six more composers. Each is built from the way that composer actually worked, not just a change of instruments. The planets are still the band, and the sky still decides when the builds and climaxes come.

  • Mozart: an eight-bar classical period, a question ending on the dominant and an answer coming home. Zeus plays an Alberti bass on the piano and Aphrodite the violin melody, composed from her sign. Gaia's oboe follows in thirds, and Hermes' flute trills at each cadence. Horns and timpani come in at the climax.
  • Satie: a slow three in the manner of the Gymnopédies. A bass note falls on the one, then a floating seventh chord, swaying between two harmonies, under a sparse, falling melody.
  • Debussy: ninth chords that glide in parallel by whole tones, harp washes from Jupiter's moons, and a flute arabesque. The Moon chooses the scale: the whole-tone scale while she waxes, the pentatonic while she wanes.
  • Pärt: tintinnabuli. The violin melody moves step by step towards a central note, in phrases that grow by one note each time, approaching alternately from above and below. The piano answers each note with the nearest note of the home triad, a cello mirrors the line below, and a bell marks the time.
  • Reich: phasing. Three marimbas play the same twelve-note pattern. The second is shifted by the angle between Venus and the Earth, the third by Mars, so as the planets move the patterns slide against each other. The clapping is phased by the Moon.
  • Glass: additive organ arpeggios that grow by a note each bar, in threes against a left hand in twos, over an organ pedal, with a choir.

The rest of the band

The Concert row has grown beyond those seven. Hildegard sings her own twelfth-century Latin words (O virtus Sapientiae, Ave generosa, O ignis Spiritus Paracliti and others) in chant composed in her manner, one human voice over a drone: a breath before each phrase, a leap of a fifth or an octave into a long melisma, a slow descent, vibrato that blooms late, and other voices joining in organum at the climax. The Latin line is shown on the floor as it is sung. Tallis is imitative polyphony, Vivaldi a concerto whose character follows the season, Chopin a nocturne with rubato, Ravel the dawn of Daphnis et Chloé: murmuring water in fast even arpeggios, birdsong from the piccolo and a solo violin, a broad melody rising slowly through the low strings and passed upward, ninth and added-sixth chords in parallel, harp glissandi, and the aerial voices of a wordless offstage chorus, far off at first and swelling to the sunrise; no snare, only a soft timpani roll and crotales. Holst gives each section to one god's movement, Shostakovich marches and grins, Messiaen uses modes of limited transposition and birdsong, Ligeti crawls through a cluster, and Morricone is in his manner only, with none of his themes: a whistle, a twanging guitar, a whip, men shouting short syllables, chimes, a wordless soprano and a mariachi trumpet, building slowly to one huge climax. Birtwistle is in his manner too, with none of his music: ritual blocks of verse and refrain, each opened by the tam-tam and heard from a new angle, over a labyrinth of pulses in which the woodblocks, toms, tuba, bass drum and the moons’ xylophones each keep their own clock (three, five, seven and eleven steps), brass cries, the horns’ refrain, an electronic aura, and the oboe’s long cantus over everything.

The Popular row is soul & gospel, funk, disco, dub and synthwave, with Aphrodite's vocal chops, slices of her recorded lines re-pitched to the sky's tune, on the song's own verse and chorus slots; switch her to Words in the band list and she sings original words about the gods in the page's language. Dub is in the manner of King Tubby and the Mad Professor: a one-drop rhythm, a deep bass, organ bubble and skank, and the mixing desk as an instrument: Ares throws the rim shot and the skank into a tape echo and a spring reverb when the sky brings an aspect, mutes the drums and bass for a bar and brings them back on the one, and Ouranos sounds the siren. The Sky mix button beside Funk lets the Moon's element choose among funk, soul and disco when a break ends; dub and synthwave are always chosen by hand, as are the club styles outside their own Sky mix.

The Avant-garde & jazz row is played in the manner of its inspirations, never quoting them. Modal jazz, after Miles Davis around Kind of Blue: one chord held for bars, the mode chosen by the Moon's element (Dorian, Aeolian, Mixolydian or Lydian), brushes and ride in swung eighths, a walking bass, piano in quartal voicings, and solos improvised from the mode by Ares' trumpet, Aphrodite's tenor and Ouranos' alto saxophone in turn, the piano taking its own in the break. The metre will not sit still: each phrase takes it from the planets' orbits, the ratio of the mean motions of two planets in aspect choosing four, three, six-eight, five or, now and then, seven, and the drums, the walking bass and the lines are made for the metre they are in. Prog rock: a suite whose metres come from the sky, the ratio of the orbital periods of the two planets in the strongest aspect choosing seven, five, nine or eleven eighths for each section, with a bass riff in unison with a Moog, Hammond and Moog solos, and Mellotron flute and choir. Moondog, after Louis Hardin: snaketime, a loping five (seven in the build and the break), home-made drums, maracas and claves, and a strict round in which flute, oboe, harpsichord and violin enter two bars apart on a theme from Aphrodite's sign. Industrial, after Throbbing Gristle: a machine kick, a metal sheet for a snare, tape loops in the moons' 1 : 2 : 4, a distorted drone, a shriek of feedback, and Ares speaking the gods' names and Greek words through a ring modulator. Ambient drift, after Space Afrika: hazy dub-techno chords in a long tape echo, hiss with rain in it, a pulse through a wall, far-off voices, and a drone, kept sparse. Zappa is in his manner (none of his music is quoted): composed lines leap in unison across xylophone, marimba and vibraphone, doubled by saxophones, brass and clavinet, while each section takes its metre (7/8, 5/8, 11/8, 9/8…) from the ratio of two planets’ orbital periods. The band stops dead, lurches for a bar into doo-wop or a lounge, Ares takes a long wah-wah guitar solo over the vamp in the build, and at the climax everyone plays a breakneck line written by the planets’ signs, in a new metre every bar. Eno is in the manner of Brian Eno’s ambient music (none of his music is quoted): each planet runs a tape loop as long as 17.8 seconds times the square root of its distance from the Sun, carrying a note or two of soft piano or a sung “aah” or “ooh”, so the loops line up as rarely as the planets do; Ouranos repeats two little cells through a long delay while the tone changes over minutes, and the sections only bring loops in and out, with no beat at all.

The World row brings music from the lands of the app’s languages, each played by the planets as the sky turns. Gagaku is the Japanese court orchestra in the tōgaku manner (no piece is quoted): Ouranos plays the shō, its clusters breathing in and out and changing on the beat, Gaia the hichiriki’s sliding melody with the ryūteki flute winding round it, Zeus and the moons the biwa and koto, and the drums keep the form, the taiko’s great stroke closing every phrase; the beat stretches in jo-ha-kyū, and the Sun’s sign picks the tonic from the twelve lü that China and Japan matched with the months. Guqin is the scholar’s seven-string zither, in the manner of the qin repertoire (no piece is quoted): Ouranos plays the harmonics (heaven), Gaia the open strings (earth) and Aphrodite the stopped melody that slides and trembles after each pluck (humanity), Hermes the low xiao flute that joins as the piece grows, and the Sun a quiet bronze bell at the phrase ends; nothing drives it, and the pentatonic’s gong is given by the Sun’s sign. Jiangnan sizhu is the “silk and bamboo” teahouse music of Shanghai, in its manner (no piece is quoted): Aphrodite’s erhu, Ares’ dizi, Ouranos’ pipa, the moons’ yangqin, Kronos’ sheng and Zeus’ sanxian all play one pentatonic tune from Aphrodite’s and Hermes’ signs at once, each ornamenting it in its own way, over Apollo’s small drum and Hermes’ clapper; slow and ornate at first, each section adds flower notes and quickens the pulse, the brighter the Moon the more flowers, to a fast climax, and the break is slow again. Nhã nhạc is the court music of Huế, framed by ca trù: in the intro and the break Aphrodite sings ca trù on vowels, throaty and bouncing with glottal catches, over Zeus’ đàn đáy lute and Hermes’ bamboo clappers, while Apollo strikes the listener’s drum in approval; then the court ensemble plays one tune together, each in its own way, led by Ares’ kèn bầu oboe. The Moon chooses the mode: bright hơi bắc while it waxes, sad hơi nam, with its bent notes, while it wanes. Oberek is the Polish village band in the fastest of the mazurka family’s dances: the leading fiddle runs and ornaments its tune over the drone of its open strings, the second fiddle chops on the off-beats, the basy bows the one, the baraban’s drum and cymbal strike the off-beats and the harmonia fills the chords, while the accent leaps to the second or third beat as Ares’ sign moves it. The Lydian raised fourth colours the tune (Aphrodite’s sign writes it), stamps and shouts of “hej!” fill the climax, and the break slows into a kujawiak. Cerdd dant is the Welsh art of singing to the harp: Apollo’s harp plays a plain air, composed by the Sun and the Moon, verse after verse, and the singer does not sing that air but sets a counter-melody of his own against it, the gosodiad, coming in a beat or more late, syncopated and led by the words, and ending with the harp; Aphrodite or Ares sings alone, then a parti of three, then a côr of four, with words from Psalm 19 in William Morgan’s Bible, Pantycelyn’s “Arglwydd, arwain” or a few plain lines about the stars, and the harp ends alone. Plygain is the Welsh carol service held before dawn by candlelight: men’s voices without any instrument, in close harmony, verse after verse, slow and plain; Apollo carries the tune in the middle, Hermes sings above it in thirds, Kronos below in sixths and open fifths, and Ares joins as the dawn comes, singing the angels’ song from Luke in William Morgan’s Bible and “Haleliwia” to tunes the Sun and the Moon compose, Dorian or Mixolydian by the Moon’s phase. Around them the church: its quiet, the candles crackling, the wind at the door, an owl, and at the climax Ouranos’ bell ringing in the dawn while Zeus’ whole company sings. Ländler is the Alpine dance band: a slow country waltz in three, Aphrodite’s zither with the tune (tremolo on the long notes) and Hermes on its chord strings, Kronos’ Steirische Harmonika with the tune in thirds, Zeus’ helicon on the one and Ares’ brass on two and three, oom-pah-pah, in tonic and dominant; Ouranos yodels, leaping a sixth or an octave between chest voice and falsetto on “hol-la-di-ri-di-o”, and in the climax comes the Schuhplattler, Gaia’s dancers slapping thighs and soles and Apollo stamping, with whoops from the moons, while Poseidon’s cowbells ring on the pasture in the intro and the break; the Sun, the Moon and the planets’ signs make the tune, the yodel and the dancers’ figures. Musette is a Paris bal-musette, in the manner of the style (no tune is quoted): a quick valse musette, lightly swung, the accordion’s three reeds tuned a little apart for the wet, shimmering tremolo that gives the style its name, running in arpeggios and chromatic passing notes over the guitar’s pompe, a banjo, the double bass and brushes. The chords follow the musette’s chains of secondary dominants (Zeus and Kronos choose them, Aphrodite’s sign writes the tune); the climax swings harder in the manouche manner, the violin answering, and the break is a slow java. Tarantella is the southern Italian dance in a fast six-eight: the tamburello’s unbroken pattern of skin and jingles is its heart, with castanets, the grunting putipù, the chitarra battente strummed on every quaver, the organetto’s bass and chords, and the mandolin’s tremolo carrying the tune Aphrodite’s sign writes. The intro belongs to the shepherds, the zampogna droning under the ciaramella; then the dance accelerates through the build and on through the climax, the frenzy said to cure the tarantula’s bite. Bulería is flamenco in its twelve-beat compás, counted from twelve and accented on 12, 3, 6, 8 and 10, a six-eight and then a three-four: palmas claras on the accents and sordas between them, the cajón, the dancer’s zapateado from the build on, and the guitar’s thumb, rasgueado and picado in the Phrygian mode, each four-compás phrase closing with the Andalusian cadence into a remate that stops dead on ten. Jaleos of “¡olé!” answer the remates, and in the break one voice sings a melismatic cante shaped by Aphrodite’s sign. Unlike the fandango, which is in three with castanets, this is the full compás.

σκηνή

On stage

Every page has a Stage button next to Record, for playing the spheres to a room.

Sound output

  • Headphones: full 3D placement of each god around you.
  • Speakers: plain left and right, for a pair of speakers.
  • PA: for a PA in a hall. The stereo is narrowed so that people standing near one speaker still hear every god.
  • Mono: everything in both speakers, for a mono PA or a single speaker.
  • 5.1: true surround sound on five speakers and a subwoofer. On the dial and in the 3D flight, each god comes from its real direction around you: planets behind you sound from the rear speakers, and turning your head turns the sky. On the dance page, the drums and bass stay at the front while the band’s gods circle the room with their planets. It needs a six-channel output: a computer connected over HDMI to a 5.1 receiver set to multichannel PCM, or a 5.1 USB sound card, in Chrome or Edge. Phones give the browser stereo only, and the button says so. Test speakers plays a chime in each speaker in turn (front left, centre, front right, rear right, rear left, then the subwoofer) so you can check the wiring.

To connect a phone to a PA, run a cable from the headphone socket, or from a USB‑C audio adapter, into two channels of the mixer. A stereo DI box stops hum on long cable runs. If the phone feeds a projector over HDMI, the sound travels down the same cable. An HDMI audio extractor gives you a line out for the mixer.

Cast and smart speakers

The music is made live on your phone or computer, by the sky as it moves, so a smart speaker plays it the way it plays any sound you send it. There is no stream on a server for the speaker to fetch, which is why there is no Alexa skill or Cast button: you send the sound from the device that is playing.

  • Bluetooth works with almost every speaker. On an Amazon Echo, say “Alexa, pair” and choose the Echo in your phone's Bluetooth settings. On a Google Nest speaker, turn on pairing in the Google Home app (the speaker's settings, then Paired Bluetooth devices). On a Panasonic SC speaker or other hi-fi, choose its Bluetooth input and pair as usual. Bluetooth adds a short delay, a fifth of a second or so. It does not matter for listening, only when you play along or send MIDI to lights.
  • Google Cast from a computer sounds better than Bluetooth, because it goes over Wi‑Fi. In Chrome or Edge on a laptop or desktop, open the ⋮ menu, choose Cast, set Sources to Cast tab, and pick a Nest speaker, a Chromecast, a TV with Chromecast built in, or any speaker that says Chromecast built-in, as many Panasonic, Sony and Bose speakers do. Keep the tab open while it plays.
  • From an Android phone, the simplest way is Bluetooth. To a Chromecast on a TV, Cast screen from the quick settings sends the picture and the sound together, which suits the projector view.
  • AirPlay: on an iPhone, iPad or Mac, choose the AirPlay button in Control Centre and pick an AirPlay speaker or Apple TV. Many Panasonic, Sonos and Bose speakers support it.

A single smart speaker is really one speaker, even if it has several drivers inside. In Stage, set the output to Mono, so that the gods panned hard to one side are not lost when the speaker folds stereo down. Two Echos or two Nest speakers set up as a stereo pair, or a stereo hi-fi, can stay on Speakers. Wi‑Fi speakers in a multi-room group add a second or two of delay. That is fine for listening, but change styles a moment early if you are timing a show to them.

MIDI out, for lights or synthesisers

In Chrome or Edge (Android, Windows, Mac or Linux, but not iPhone), press Connect MIDI and choose a device. A USB MIDI interface, a MIDI-to-DMX box or a lighting desk with MIDI in all work.

  • Each god plays on its own channel at its real pitch: 1 Helios (Sun), 2 Hermes, 3 Aphrodite, 4 Gaia, 5 Ares, 6 Zeus, 7 Kronos, 8 Ouranos, 9 Poseidon, 10 Jupiter's moons, 11 everything else. On the dial, the drones are held notes, and their loudness is sent as CC 20 on their own channel.
  • Channel 16 carries the shape of the show. CC 20 is the section: intro 0, groove 32, build 64, drop 96, break 127. CC 21 is the overall loudness. A note marks each section change: 36 intro, 37 groove, 38 build, 39 drop, 40 break.
  • Send MIDI clock sends the dance tempo (24 pulses a beat), with start and stop.

Projector view

This fills the screen with the dial, the dance or the 3D flight, and hides the controls. A small button in the corner, or Esc, brings them back. Keep the screen awake stops the phone going to sleep mid-show. Double-tap the sky or the score to go straight to the projector view, and double-tap again to come back.

Install it as an app

In Chrome, choose Install app or Add to Home screen. The app then opens full screen and keeps a copy of every page and instrument, so it plays without a signal. It updates itself whenever there is a connection.

φῶς

Light and radio

Light and sound are both vibrations, and an octave means the same for both: twice the frequency. Lower a colour of light by forty octaves, dividing its frequency by 240 (about 1.1 million million), and it lands in hearing. The A above middle C, 440 Hz, raised forty octaves is 4.84 × 1014 Hz: light of 620 nm, an orange-red. The whole of visible light, from violet at 380 nm to deep red at about 750 nm, spans less than one octave; the ear hears about ten. This one rule, forty octaves, is used for the two light layers below. Light, in the Choir section of the dial and in the flight's Navigation panel, has three switches: Colour is on by default, Heat and Radio are off, and the choice is remembered. Tap a body to hear it alone and its light layers come with it, and the information card shows a line such as “Mars · reflects red, absorbs blue · heat 14 µm → a 19.8 Hz pulse”.

Colour: the planet's reflected light shapes its voice

Each body has a measured reflectance spectrum: how much of each wavelength it sends back, from the near ultraviolet at 350 nm to the near infrared at 1000 nm. Brought down forty octaves, that span runs from about 780 Hz to 270 Hz, and it becomes a filter on the body's voice, lifting the colours it reflects and cutting those it absorbs, by up to 12 dB. A voice's harmonics cover many octaves and the light covers one and a half, so the curve is folded by octaves the way notes are folded into pitch classes: the visible octave, 380–760 nm, is the reference, a near-infrared wavelength is halved into it (890 nm sits with 445 nm, as a note an octave down keeps its name) and the near ultraviolet is doubled. Each octave of light is set about its own mean first, so the infrared adds its bands without darkening the blue it folds onto, and the one-octave curve is then applied to every octave of the voice. Mars, which reflects red and absorbs blue, becomes warmer and darker. Uranus and Neptune take audible notches where methane absorbs, at 619 nm, 727 nm and 890 nm (and 543 nm for Neptune): the 619 nm band lands almost exactly on A440. Jupiter's and Saturn's weaker methane bands and Venus's bright yellowish clouds each colour their voices. The asteroids and dwarf planets use class spectra (a dark grey C-type, a reddish S-type, bright ice, cold red bodies) and comets the dark red of a comet's nucleus. On phones the curve is smoothed more coarsely, and in both cases it is applied as one short filter rather than a rack of equalisers. It changes the timbre, not the loudness: a flat voice passes at the same level.

Heat: the planet's own warmth as a bass or a throb

Every body glows in the infrared, and Wien's law gives the wavelength where it glows most: 2898 µm·K divided by its temperature. Forty octaves down, that peak becomes a pitch. Earth, at 255 K, peaks at 11.4 µm, which is 24 Hz: a sine hum at the bottom of hearing, with a quieter second harmonic so that a phone's small speaker can hear it too. Mercury (440 K on average; its day side reaches 700 K) hums at 41 Hz and Venus's cloud tops (230 K, though its surface is 737 K) at 22 Hz. Below 20 Hz the peak is too slow to hear as a note, so it becomes a throb, an amplitude pulse at that rate on a soft low drone a few octaves up: Mars (210 K) throbs at 19.8 Hz, Jupiter (124 K) at 11.7 Hz, Saturn (95 K) at 8.9 Hz, Uranus and Neptune (59 K) at 5.6 Hz, Pluto (44 K) at 4.1 Hz. The loudness follows the body's thermal output as seen from where you are listening, compressed into musical limits; Jupiter radiates 1.7 times the energy it receives from the Sun, Saturn 1.8 and Neptune 2.6, so their heat is relatively stronger. The layer is kept quiet, goes through the same automatic gain and limiter as the choir, and is left out of the score and MIDI.

Radio: the planets that transmit

Some planets send out real radio waves, and radio astronomers listen to them through a receiver, which turns them into sound by demodulation, not by lowering their pitch: there is no forty-octave rule here, and the textures are a receiver's noise, not tones. Each one follows the real sky of the moment. Jupiter's decametric storms (10–40 MHz) depend on where Io is in its orbit and on which side of Jupiter faces you: the Io-A, B, C and D source regions, roughly as catalogued, are computed from the app's Io and from Jupiter's System III rotation of 9 h 55 m 29.7 s, corrected for light time. In a storm you hear L-bursts, a slow surf of noise, and S-bursts, rapid crackles and pops; between storms a quieter hectometric hiss. Saturn's kilometric radiation is whistling chirps and squeaks that wax and wane with its 10.7-hour rotation. Earth's auroral kilometric radiation is rising and falling chirps, like the whistlers and chorus of the magnetosphere, stronger from the night side and in active spells, whose weather here is random. Uranus and Neptune give faint short bursts in one sector of their rotations, 17.2 and 16.1 hours. Radio is heard only where the planet's voice is heard, scaled to it, and never enters the score.

Tips and troubleshooting

  • Wear headphones. Positions, elevation and the turning of the choir as you move are much clearer.
  • No sound? Browsers only allow sound after you tap, so press Begin the harmony, Play or a planet. Check that your phone isn't on silent.
  • Inside the Claude app, the embedded preview may block sound on some phones. Open the web links in your phone's browser instead.
  • Crackling on a phone means the phone is running short of power for audio. The app already sings fewer voices on phones. If you still hear it, turn off Gods introduce themselves and, in 3D, leave Headphone 3D sound off.
  • Record captures exactly what you hear on any of the three pages. Press it to start (it starts the sound too) and again to stop. You can then play the recording back and save it as an audio file (.m4a on most phones and computers, .webm on some) to listen to anywhere. Spoken introductions come from your device's speech voice, so they are not included.
  • Each page remembers its own volume, so the 2D dial, 3D flight and dance can sit at different levels.
  • Speeding up time brings out the glides, the moons' melodies and the turning of the key through the year.
  • The first version is kept unchanged as a benchmark: v1 dial and v1 flight.
ἀκρίβεια

Data, accuracy and reading

  • Planet positions use NASA JPL's approximate Keplerian elements, valid to a fraction of a degree from about 1800 to 2050.
  • Dwarf planets, asteroids and comets use rounded elements, so their positions are illustrative rather than exact.
  • Only major moons are included, on circular orbits in their planet's equatorial plane. Moon orbits on the dial are not to scale.
  • The zodiac constellations are drawn from the catalogued positions of their main stars, converted to the ecliptic. The key follows the tropical signs, measured from the spring equinox, as astrologers use them.
  • On the 2D dial, distances are logarithmic so that Mercury and the Kuiper belt fit on one screen.
